# “Chat Control” in the European Union

## A legal, legislative, technical and political history of the interim and permanent regimes

**Research cut-off:** 14 July 2026

**Jurisdiction:** European Union

**Files:** 2020/0259(COD), 2023/0452(COD), 2025/0429(COD), and 2022/0155(COD)

> **Short answer.** “Chat Control” is a campaigning label, not the title of one EU law. It usually refers to two related but legally distinct projects. **Chat Control 1.0** was a temporary ePrivacy derogation that let covered communication providers *voluntarily* scan communications for child sexual abuse material (CSAM) and grooming. It applied from 2 August 2021 to 3 April 2026, but is currently lapsed. A second extension is still unfinished after an extraordinary July 2026 second reading. **Chat Control 2.0** is the proposed permanent Child Sexual Abuse Regulation (CSAR). The Commission’s 2022 version included mandatory, authority-issued detection orders. Parliament’s 2023 position made those orders targeted and excluded end-to-end encrypted communications. The Council’s November 2025 mandate deleted the detection-order machinery and would instead make voluntary scanning permanent. Five trilogues have not resolved the central detection question. Neither version is a presently operative permanent EU “mass-scanning law.”

## 1. Scope, terminology and method

### What “both versions” means

This report uses the common two-part taxonomy:

- **Chat Control 1.0:** Regulation (EU) 2021/1232, the temporary derogation from parts of the ePrivacy Directive; its 2024 extension, Regulation (EU) 2024/1307; and the unfinished second extension, COM(2025) 797.
- **Chat Control 2.0:** COM(2022) 209, the proposed permanent regulation laying down rules to prevent and combat child sexual abuse, usually called the **CSAR**.

That taxonomy is useful but unofficial. Critics coined “chat control” to emphasize the scanning of private communications. EU institutions generally use “interim regulation,” “temporary ePrivacy derogation,” “online child sexual abuse,” or “CSAR.” A careful analysis must not collapse four different things into one:

1. permission for a private provider to scan voluntarily;
2. a legal duty imposed on a provider;
3. a targeted order issued by a court or independent authority; and
4. indiscriminate scanning of every user and every message.

Different institutional texts have proposed different combinations. A headline saying that “the EU passed Chat Control” is therefore incomplete unless it identifies the file, stage, institution and exact legal effect.

### Method and evidentiary rules

The report prioritizes enacted texts, EUR-Lex, Parliament’s Legislative Observatory and voting records, Council documents and voting records, Commission reports, data-protection authorities, and peer-reviewed or signed technical analyses. It distinguishes:

- **adopted law** from **institutional negotiating positions**;
- **recorded votes** from political discussions, straw polls, postponed agenda items and consensus decisions without a public roll call;
- **reports** from **files or images** (one report may contain many files; many files may be duplicates); and
- an institution’s claim from an independently established fact.

The cut-off matters. The Parliament vote on 9 July 2026 did **not** finish the second extension. As of 14 July, the amended position was awaiting the Council.

## 2. The legal problem the EU was trying to solve

### Why an ePrivacy derogation became necessary

Private providers had long used technologies such as hash matching and classifiers to find CSAM and grooming on some services. The European Electronic Communications Code expanded the ePrivacy framework to “number-independent interpersonal communications services” such as webmail and messaging. From 21 December 2020, the confidentiality rule in Article 5(1) and restrictions on processing traffic data in Article 6 of the ePrivacy Directive created uncertainty over whether providers could continue scanning communications on their own initiative.

The Commission responded on 10 September 2020 with [COM(2020) 568](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52020PC0568): a narrow, temporary permission to continue specified voluntary practices while permanent legislation was prepared. The result was [Regulation (EU) 2021/1232](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2021/1232/oj/eng).

### The rights in tension

The legislative objective is exceptionally important: preventing sexual violence, stopping repeated circulation of abuse imagery, identifying children in immediate danger, and investigating offenders. The Charter protects children’s best interests and their right to protection and care (Article 24), and public authorities have positive obligations to protect victims.

The means interfere with other Charter rights:

- respect for private and family life and communications (Article 7);
- protection of personal data (Article 8);
- freedom of expression and information (Article 11);
- non-discrimination (Article 21);
- children’s privacy and development rights as well as their protection (Article 24); and
- an effective remedy and fair hearing (Article 47).

Under Article 52(1), limitations must be provided by law, respect the essence of the rights, pursue a legitimate objective and be necessary and proportionate. The core dispute is not whether protecting children is legitimate. It is whether a particular detection design is sufficiently precise, targeted, effective and safeguarded to justify examining enormous volumes of innocent communications.

The Court of Justice has not yet ruled on the CSAR. Analogies must therefore be used carefully. Its communications-data cases establish a demanding baseline: legislation interfering with Articles 7 and 8 needs clear rules and minimum safeguards; generalized access to the *content* of communications can compromise the essence of privacy; and even general and indiscriminate retention of traffic and location data for ordinary serious-crime purposes is impermissible, whereas targeted measures may be possible. See [Digital Rights Ireland, C‑293/12 and C‑594/12](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62012CJ0293), [Schrems I, C‑362/14](https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-362/14), and [Tele2 Sverige, C‑203/15 and C‑698/15](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2016-12/cp160145en.pdf). These cases are highly relevant principles, not a pre-judgment that every possible CSAM regime is unlawful.

## 3. Chat Control 1.0: the temporary voluntary regime

### What the 2021 regulation did

[Regulation (EU) 2021/1232](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2021/1232/oj/eng) created a temporary derogation from Articles 5(1) and 6(1) of the ePrivacy Directive for providers of **number-independent interpersonal communications services**. In practical terms, that includes many messaging, chat, internet-telephony and webmail services. It did not itself command a provider to scan. It allowed providers to use specified technologies voluntarily to:

- detect and remove **known CSAM**;
- detect and remove **previously unidentified (“new”) CSAM**;
- detect **solicitation of children**, commonly called grooming; and
- report suspected abuse to law-enforcement authorities or organizations acting in the public interest against child sexual abuse.

The derogation concerns the ePrivacy confidentiality rules. It does **not** exempt providers from the GDPR. Providers still need a lawful basis and must comply with data-protection principles. The distinction matters: saying that the regulation “legalized scanning” is shorthand for removing one ePrivacy obstacle, not for creating an unlimited exemption from all EU privacy law.

The regulation excluded audio communications. It did not expressly exclude end-to-end encrypted services. Because it was technology-neutral, its legal coverage and the technical feasibility of applying it to an encrypted service were separate questions.

### Safeguards in the enacted text

Article 3 imposed cumulative conditions. Among the most important:

- processing had to be strictly necessary and proportionate to the sole purpose of combating online child sexual abuse;
- technologies had to be state of the art, least privacy-intrusive, sufficiently reliable and designed to minimize error rates;
- text scanning could detect only patterns pointing to possible abuse and could not deduce the substance of the communication;
- technologies for grooming and previously unidentified material required prior consultation with the competent supervisory authority and a data-protection impact assessment;
- unknown-CSAM and grooming matches could not be reported without prior human confirmation;
- providers had to offer effective, accessible complaint and redress mechanisms and inform users when content was removed or an account blocked;
- data were generally to be erased immediately, with tightly enumerated retention purposes and a maximum retention period of 12 months; and
- providers and Member States had annual transparency-reporting duties.

Known-CSAM matching could rely on unambiguous identifiers such as cryptographic or robust perceptual hashes. The law also required human oversight, procedures to prevent abuse, internal records, and reporting to supervisory authorities.

### What it did *not* do

The interim regulation did not:

- require every provider to participate;
- empower police to issue detection orders under this instrument;
- create the proposed EU Centre;
- establish a general EU age-verification duty;
- authorize unrelated content scanning; or
- suspend the GDPR.

Nonetheless, voluntariness does not eliminate the rights impact. A participating provider can still process communications of every user in the relevant service, including people under no suspicion. The decisive proportionality questions therefore remain the scope of the provider’s deployment, the technique used, accuracy, human review, redress and downstream reporting.

### The original sunset

The regulation entered into force on 2 August 2021 and was originally due to cease applying on 3 August 2024. It was deliberately described as temporary while the Commission developed a permanent framework.

## 4. The 2024 extension

By late 2023, neither Parliament nor the Council was close to agreeing on the permanent CSAR. On 30 November 2023 the Commission proposed [COM(2023) 777](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52023PC0777), a two-year extension of the interim regime.

Parliament did not give the Commission a blank renewal. The LIBE committee’s 31 January 2024 position proposed a one-off extension only to 3 May 2025 and sought stronger transparency. Negotiators reached a different compromise on 15 February: expiry on 3 April 2026 and a standardized format for provider reports. The final [Regulation (EU) 2024/1307](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1307/oj/eng) amended the sunset and reporting provisions but did not fundamentally redesign the scanning safeguards.

The law was signed on 29 April 2024, published on 14 May and applied from 15 May. The dates left no actual gap before the earlier August 2024 sunset.

Independent oversight remained strongly critical. In [Opinion 8/2024](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/opinions/2024-01-24-edps-opinion-82024-regulation-amending-regulation-eu-20211232-temporary-derogation-certain-eprivacy-provisions-combating-csam_en), the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS) said the 2020 recommendations had not been fully addressed, that effective protection against general and indiscriminate monitoring was still missing, and that the observed error risks—especially for grooming and consensually produced imagery—made extension more than a formality. It recommended not adopting the extension without necessary safeguards. The co-legislators adopted it nonetheless.

## 5. The failed 2025–26 extension and July 2026 revival

This episode explains why apparently contradictory July 2026 headlines can all quote real numbers.

### Commission proposal: extend to April 2028

On 19 December 2025 the Commission proposed [COM(2025) 797](https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/document/download/12f38698-ffd9-4cdb-acf5-30d1a3b92441_en?filename=Proposal+to+extend+Interim+Regulation.pdf), another extension, this time to 3 April 2028. The stated purpose was to avoid a gap while the permanent CSAR remained under negotiation.

The EDPS’s [Opinion 7/2026](https://www.edps.europa.eu/system/files/2026-02/26-02-16_opinion-extending-application-regulation-2021-1232_en.pdf) again accepted child protection as an objective of general interest but identified two unresolved defects: no sufficiently clear GDPR legal basis for the processing, and insufficient safeguards against general and indiscriminate scanning. It treated the extension as an opportunity to repair both.

### LIBE rejected its own report

The committee process was not a simple yes/no vote on scanning. Amendments and compromise packages attempted to narrow the regime, but on 2 March 2026 LIBE rejected the resulting draft report by **28 votes in favor, 38 against and 3 abstentions**. Under Parliament’s procedure, the committee therefore tabled a recommendation to reject the Commission proposal. A vote “for” at this stage meant support for the amended report, not necessarily support for the Commission’s unamended text.

### Parliament first adopted a narrow mandate, then rejected the proposal

On 11 March, plenary instead adopted a narrowly amended negotiating position by **458–103–63** and referred it back for negotiations. It would have:

- ended the interim regime on 3 August 2027;
- limited proactive matching principally to known CSAM;
- allowed unknown-CSAM and grooming detection only after a concrete user, trusted-flagger or public-interest notification concerning a specific communication, account or interaction;
- required an independent judicial authorization tied to a reasonable suspicion and a specific target;
- excluded communications protected, formerly protected or intended to be protected by end-to-end encryption; and
- excluded traffic data from the derogation.

The exact first-reading amendments are in Parliament’s [11 March adopted text](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-10-2026-0070_EN.html).

Negotiators did not bridge the difference with the Council. On 26 March, Parliament completed its first reading by rejecting the Commission proposal: **228 voted for the proposal, 311 against and 92 abstained**. The operative point is the outcome, not the misleading shorthand that Parliament had “voted against child protection.” Parliament rejected *that legal design after failed negotiations*.

The existing Regulation 2021/1232 then expired on **3 April 2026**. Providers no longer had this Union-level ePrivacy derogation. EUR-Lex accordingly marks the consolidated act as no longer in force.

### Council revived the file at first reading

After Parliament President Roberta Metsola urged the European Council on 18 June to find a political agreement, the Council adopted a first-reading position by written procedure on 2 July. Its [position and statement of reasons](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/07/02/council-moves-to-reinstate-interim-measure-to-combat-child-sexual-abuse-online/) would restore the broadly existing voluntary regime until 3 April 2028.

The Council vote was **27–0–0**. Italy supported the position but attached a statement emphasizing that the extension was exceptional and temporary and calling, in the permanent file, for proportionality, a focus on known CSAM, judicial oversight and protection of end-to-end encryption. The official result and statement appear in [Council document ST 11513/26](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-11513-2026-INIT/en/pdf).

This was procedurally unusual but lawful. Under Article 294 TFEU, a Council first-reading position can go to Parliament even after Parliament has rejected the Commission proposal at first reading; Parliament then has three months to approve, reject or amend it at second reading.

### The 7 July urgency vote

Parliament first had to decide whether to handle the Council position urgently. On 7 July it approved the urgent procedure by **331–304–11**. That vote accelerated consideration; it did not approve the substantive law. The official numbers appear in the [7 July plenary vote record](https://data.europarl.europa.eu/distribution/reds_iPlPv_Vot/PV-10-2026-07-07-VOT/PV-10-2026-07-07-VOT-FNL_en.pdf).

### The 9 July second reading—and why 314–276 did not reject the text

At second reading, Article 294(7) TFEU requires a majority of Parliament’s **component members** to reject or amend the Council position. Parliament stated that the threshold was then **360**, regardless of how many members voted.

1. A motion to reject the Council position received **314 for rejection, 276 against rejection and 17 abstentions**. Although more participating MEPs supported rejection, 314 was below 360, so the motion failed.
2. Parliament then voted on amendments. Two amendment votes cleared the 360 threshold. They inserted an exclusion for communications to which end-to-end encryption “is, has been or will be applied.” The adopted text is [T10-0266/2026](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-10-2026-0266_EN.html).
3. A final Rule 68(4) motion to reject the *amended* position received **276 for rejection, 286 against and 30 abstentions** and therefore failed.

This is why “314 voted to reject, but Parliament did not reject” is not a contradiction. The controlling threshold was an absolute majority of all seats, not a simple majority of votes cast. Parliament’s own [press release explains the threshold and next steps](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20260706IPR46318/combating-child-sexual-abuse-support-for-a-more-limited-eprivacy-derogation).

### Status on 14 July 2026

Parliament’s second reading is closed. The amended position has gone to the Council, which has three months to accept **all** Parliament amendments. If it does, the act can be adopted. If it does not, a six-week conciliation committee must seek a joint text; failure in conciliation kills the proposal.

There is therefore still a legal gap. Regulation 2021/1232 remains expired, and the July text is not yet law. The current status is **pending Council acceptance or conciliation**, not “extended until 2028.”

## 6. What implementation data actually show

### The evidence problem is itself a major finding

The interim regulation required providers and Member States to report, in part so legislators could test necessity and proportionality. The two Commission implementation reports instead reveal weak and non-comparable evidence.

The [first report, COM(2023) 797](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52023DC0797), found different reporting periods, incomplete Member-State submissions and inconsistent provider metrics. It noted that 49% of NCMEC reports in 2022 were classified as actionable and 51% as informational, but that distinction is not the same as true versus false or guilty versus innocent.

The [second report, COM(2025) 740](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52025DC0740), covering 2023 and 2024, was more explicit:

- some providers did not use the standard form;
- several Member States submitted late, partial or no data;
- companies used different denominators and definitions;
- some data were global rather than EU-specific;
- no reliable Union-wide total could be calculated for reports, children identified or perpetrators convicted; and
- providers did not supply enough information to assess whether tools were state of the art, least intrusive, subject to the required impact assessment or preceded by supervisory consultation.

The Commission concluded that the available information was **insufficient to answer definitively whether the derogation was proportionate**. It nevertheless said it had no indications of disproportionality and pointed to thousands of children reportedly identified and millions of items removed. The first sentence is an evidentiary limit; the second is the Commission’s policy judgment under that limit. They should not be merged into a claim that proportionality was empirically proven.

### Provider figures for 2023 and 2024

The following table reproduces the report’s materially comparable figures, with all caveats intact.

| Provider | 2023 reported detections/actions | 2024 reported detections/actions | Important limitation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Google | 1,558 items; 734 NCMEC reports; 635 accounts | 1,824 items; 508 NCMEC reports | A report can include multiple items; account and item counts differ. |
| LinkedIn | 2 items | 1 item | Scanned roughly 24m images + 1m videos in 2023 and 22m + 2m in 2024 in the EU. |
| Meta | about 3.6m items | about 1.5m items | Provider metric; not the number of unique victims or crimes. |
| Microsoft | about 9,000 EU items | more than 5,800 EU items | It reported processing 11.7bn and 9.6bn items globally, without an EU denominator. |
| Yubo | 7,720: 2 known-CSAM, 938 new-CSAM, 6,780 grooming/exploitation | 4,484 | Very different mix from hash-matching services. |

The enormous denominator difference matters. LinkedIn reported tens of millions of EU images and videos processed; Microsoft reported billions globally. A small error rate applied at that scale can generate many reviews, while a low number of confirmed matches can mean either excellent precision, weak recall, a low prevalence, or some combination. The public data do not distinguish these possibilities.

### Accuracy and redress figures cannot be compared at face value

Reported aggregate ratios included approximately:

- Google hash matching: 18 erroneous matches out of 1,576 in 2023 (1.14%) and 10 out of 1,834 in 2024 (0.54%);
- Meta: 0.32% and 0.12%, calculated through restored content or reversed actions rather than a common validation test;
- Yubo new-CSAM/grooming systems: about 20% and 13%; and
- Microsoft: no usable error-rate data.

These are not standardized false-positive rates. Some use appeals as the numerator, some corrections, and some provider validation. They say little about **false negatives**, because undetected abuse has no record in the system.

Redress volumes are also revealing but ambiguous. Meta reported about 254,500 appeals in 2023, with roughly 11,600 restorations or reversals, and about 76,900 appeals with 1,800 reversals in 2024. Google reported 297 appeals and 10 accounts restored in 2023, then 216 and 19. Yubo reported 1,159 appeals and 50 reversals, then 31 and none. An appeal may be unsuccessful because the original action was sound; low appeal use may also reflect weak notice or accessibility. The numbers are neither proof of systemic error nor proof of reliable redress.

### Reporting destination and EU autonomy

All reporting providers sent reports to the US-based National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC); Yubo also used the Internet Watch Foundation and France’s PHAROS. This reflects the present global reporting ecosystem but raises questions about dependence on a US organization, onward transfers, EU procedural standards and how duplicates or low-quality reports are filtered. One purpose of the proposed EU Centre is to receive and validate EU reports before forwarding actionable material to national authorities and Europol.

### What can responsibly be concluded

The data support four modest conclusions:

1. participating providers are processing very large volumes and do identify material or interactions that they treat as serious enough to report or remove;
2. known-CSAM hash matching is a technically different and generally more determinate task than classifying new imagery or grooming language;
3. erroneous actions and successful appeals occur, sometimes at meaningful absolute scale; and
4. the reporting system has not generated the standardized outcome evidence needed for a robust EU-wide cost-benefit or proportionality assessment.

Claims that the interim regime “saved no children” and claims that it has “proved proportionate” both go beyond the published evidence.

## 7. Chat Control 2.0: the permanent CSAR proposal

### Commission proposal of 11 May 2022

The Commission’s [COM(2022) 209](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52022PC0209) is much broader than the interim derogation. It would create a permanent regulatory system covering:

- hosting services;
- publicly available interpersonal communications services;
- software application stores; and
- internet-access services for blocking-order purposes.

Its architecture has five layers.

#### 1. Risk assessment and mitigation

Providers would assess how their services could be used for online child sexual abuse, identify design and user-risk factors, and adopt proportionate mitigation. Measures could include safer defaults, reporting tools, moderation, age-related safeguards and changes to recommendation or contact features. National coordinating authorities would supervise compliance.

#### 2. Detection orders

If mitigation remained insufficient and evidence indicated a significant risk, the coordinating authority could ask a judicial or independent administrative authority to issue a detection order. Under the Commission text, an order could cover:

- known CSAM;
- new CSAM; and
- solicitation of children.

Known- and new-CSAM orders could last up to 24 months; grooming orders up to 12 months. Providers would use technologies meeting statutory requirements and indicators supplied through the EU Centre. The Commission described orders as a last resort, risk-specific and subject to independent authorization. Critics focused on the fact that a service-level order could still cause automated examination of communications belonging to very many people not individually suspected.

#### 3. Reporting, removal, blocking and delisting

Providers finding apparent abuse would report to the EU Centre. Hosting providers could be ordered to remove or disable access to identified CSAM, ordinarily within 24 hours. Blocking orders could require internet-access providers to block listed URLs where removal at source was not feasible. Later Council drafts also developed search-engine delisting orders. Users, providers and content owners would have notice, complaint and judicial-redress rights, subject to investigation-related delays.

#### 4. A new EU Centre

The decentralized **EU Centre to Prevent and Combat Child Sexual Abuse**, proposed for The Hague beside Europol, would:

- maintain databases of indicators;
- receive and assess provider reports;
- discard reports that are manifestly unfounded;
- forward potentially actionable reports to Europol and competent national authorities;
- support providers, victims and national authorities;
- facilitate technology testing; and
- collect transparency data.

The hoped-for gain is a specialized EU filter between private-company detections and police. The controversy concerns its independence, its access to highly sensitive material, cooperation with Europol and non-EU bodies, indicator governance and the possibility that a filtering agency normalizes high-volume surveillance.

#### 5. Enforcement

Member States would designate coordinating and other competent authorities. Maximum penalties for providers could reach **6% of worldwide annual turnover**. The regulation would generally apply six months after entry into force.

### Encryption in the Commission proposal

The Commission text did not expressly order providers to “break encryption” and described itself as technology-neutral. It also did not exclude end-to-end encrypted communications from detection orders. Those two facts can coexist.

True end-to-end encryption means only the communicating endpoints possess the keys needed to read content. Server-side scanning cannot inspect that plaintext. A provider could comply only through a technique that changes the security model—for example, analyzing content on the device before encryption or after decryption, or altering key/access arrangements. Calling this “scanning without breaking encryption” is semantically possible because the transport cipher can remain intact; security experts object that it defeats the protection users reasonably expect from E2EE by adding a privileged content-inspection capability at an endpoint.

### Parliament’s November 2023 mandate

LIBE adopted rapporteur Javier Zarzalejos’s heavily amended report on 14 November 2023 by **51–2–1**, and authorized negotiations by **48–2–4**. The mandate became final on 22 November because no political group or sufficient number of MEPs objected by the plenary deadline. There was **no separate plenary roll-call vote** on the substance.

Parliament retained risk assessment, mitigation, removal, blocking, reporting, victim support and an EU Centre, but fundamentally narrowed detection:

- no general monitoring;
- detection as a last resort;
- prior validation by a judicial authority;
- targeting of an individual user or a specific group of users for whom there were reasonable grounds to suspect a link to CSAM;
- exclusion of end-to-end encrypted communications;
- stronger complaints, remedies and transparency; and
- restrictions on age-assurance measures and sensitive data.

The full position is Parliament report [A9-0364/2023](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/A-9-2023-0364_EN.html); the [LIBE announcement](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20231113IPR09917/child-sexual-abuse-online-effective-measures-no-mass-surveillance) summarizes the political bargain.

### The Council’s long search for a mandate

The Council did not take one continuous up-or-down vote on the Commission proposal. Rotating presidencies circulated compromise texts, tested support and repeatedly declined to put an unready text to a formal vote.

#### Czech Presidency, July–December 2022

The Law Enforcement Working Party examined the whole proposal in eight meetings. The Presidency moved removal, blocking and proposed delisting orders toward competent national authorities while reserving the most intrusive detection orders for a court or independent administrative authority. Two expert workshops examined detection technologies and age verification. Its [December 2022 progress report](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-14862-2022-INIT/en/pdf) recorded broad political support for the objective but serious proportionality concerns.

#### Swedish Presidency, January–June 2023

The EDPS presented its concerns to the working party in January. Parliament’s complementary impact assessment appeared in April. On 26 April the Council Legal Service issued Opinion 8787/23. It concluded that applying the proposed detection orders to interpersonal communications was not sufficiently precise or Charter-compliant and advised narrowing them to users reasonably believed to be involved in or connected with abuse. Coreper held a policy debate on 31 May; technical drafting continued, but no mandate emerged.

The Legal Service opinion is important but is not a court judgment. It was internal legal advice to the Council. The official [Opinion 8787/23](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-8787-2023-INIT/en/pdf) provides the analysis.

#### Spanish Presidency, July–December 2023

Spain tried to make detection a last resort, target “specific types of users” where possible, improve encryption safeguards and logging, and strengthen the EU Centre’s testing role. When delegations still objected, it proposed initially limiting detection orders to known CSAM and delaying new-CSAM and grooming provisions until technologies were sufficiently reliable.

Coreper considered that approach on 13 October but it lacked sufficient support. Ministers discussed the file on 19 October and 5 December. The [Spanish progress report](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-16098-2023-INIT/en/pdf) states plainly that no qualified-majority compromise had been found.

#### Belgian Presidency, January–June 2024

Belgium sought a “user consent” or “upload moderation” compromise focused on images, video and URLs shared through high-risk services. The central objection was that consent to scanning offered as a condition for sending a message is not necessarily freely given, and on-device upload analysis still creates a scanning capability. The Council’s [June progress report](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-10666-2024-INIT/en/pdf) counted 31 working-party meetings and recorded continuing division. A planned decision was not taken because a qualified majority was not assured.

#### Hungarian Presidency, July–December 2024

Hungary continued work on upload moderation and discussed a partial general approach in December. That text still failed to secure the required majority. It is more accurate to say that no formal adoption vote was held than to assign “yes” or “no” votes to every country from press leaks or advocacy trackers.

#### Polish Presidency, January–June 2025

Poland reorganized the text around risk categories and possible mandatory mitigation, while the detection dispute persisted. The Council received another progress report in June. No mandate was adopted.

#### Danish Presidency and the breakthrough, July–November 2025

Denmark initially revived mandatory detection. In November it changed strategy: delete Articles 7–11—the mandatory detection-order system—from the Council mandate, make the interim voluntary-scanning permission permanent, and require a future review of whether detection orders were necessary and technically feasible. That narrower package secured agreement in Coreper on **26 November 2025**.

The Council did not publish a country-by-country roll call for the Coreper consensus. It calls the result a “position” or “partial negotiating mandate,” not an adopted regulation. The [Council announcement](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2025/11/26/child-sexual-abuse-council-reaches-position-on-law-protecting-children-from-online-abuse/) and [mandate ST 15318/25](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-15318-2025-INIT/en/pdf) are the reliable sources.

The Council mandate:

- classifies services as high, medium or low risk;
- requires risk assessment and proportionate mitigation;
- allows authorities to require high-risk providers to develop mitigating technologies;
- deletes mandatory detection obligations and says the regulation itself imposes none;
- makes voluntary scanning under the logic of Regulation 2021/1232 permanent;
- protects encryption and cybersecurity in Article 1(5);
- retains reporting, removal, blocking and delisting mechanisms and the EU Centre; and
- requires a review within three years on the need and feasibility of detection orders.

The seat of the EU Centre was excluded from the partial mandate for a separate interinstitutional selection process.

## 11. The case for the legislation

The strongest case is not “privacy versus children.” Children need both safety and confidential communications. The serious pro-regulation argument is that voluntary action and fragmented national law leave predictable gaps that offenders exploit.

### Abuse is severe, networked and persistent

CSAM records a crime and its redistribution can repeatedly victimize the child. Grooming can move rapidly from contact to coercion, extortion, live-streamed abuse or an offline meeting. Large services operate across borders, while investigations and takedowns remain nationally fragmented. The Commission cited **85 million images and videos** reported worldwide in 2021; that figure describes files, many of them duplicates, not 85 million victims or investigations. It still demonstrates enormous distribution volume. See the Commission’s [proposal announcement](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_22_2976).

### Known-material matching can stop repeated circulation

Where vetted authorities have already classified an image or video as illegal, hash matching can identify copies without a semantic judgment about every photograph. Robust hashes can also recognize some resizing or recompression. Removing these copies reduces repeated exposure and can connect previously unknown accounts or services to a known series.

This is the technically strongest part of the detection case. It still depends on a sound reference database, collision/error control, secure indicator handling, human escalation and remedies, but it does not present the same open-ended inference problem as asking a model to decide whether a novel image or conversation is abusive.

### New-material and grooming detection may identify ongoing abuse

Known-hash systems find only material already discovered and classified. A regime limited to known material can miss a child whose abuse has never been seen. Grooming classifiers aim to find coercive behavioral patterns before abuse escalates. Child-protection organizations and law enforcement therefore argue that new-CSAM and grooming capabilities are indispensable rather than optional extras.

The [Europol/European Chief of Police statement](https://www.europol.europa.eu/media-press/newsroom/news/joint-declaration-of-european-police-chiefs) argues that loss of access to communications content impedes investigation and victim rescue. The [ECLAG/Eurochild position](https://www.eclag.eu/what-we-do/eu-regulation-to-prevent-and-combat-child-sexual-abuse) stresses victims’ need for durable takedown, prevention and a specialized EU Centre. These are stakeholder positions, but they articulate real operational gaps.

### Harmonization can improve due process

A well-designed EU law could be better than opaque, provider-specific voluntary practice. It could establish:

- judicial thresholds;
- audited technologies and vetted indicators;
- common error and outcome metrics;
- provider and authority transparency;
- notice, appeal and judicial remedy;
- victim rights to find and remove material;
- consistent cross-border orders; and
- an EU body that filters low-quality reports before police receive them.

This point is often missed: rejecting the Commission’s detection-order design does not necessarily preserve a world without scanning. It may preserve private, nonuniform scanning with weaker public accountability. That is one reason the Council moved toward permanent voluntary scanning and one reason critics say “voluntary” is not an adequate permanent rights framework.

### Risk mitigation and safety by design are valuable even without scanning

The least controversial CSAR elements are preventive: assess product risk, restrict unsolicited adult contact with children, give minors safe defaults, make block/report tools obvious, moderate high-risk discovery features, respond rapidly to victim requests and make app stores address risks. Those duties can reduce opportunities for abuse before content detection is considered.

### A focused EU Centre could add value

If independent, secure and tightly governed, the EU Centre could reduce duplicate reports, distinguish actionable cases, support smaller providers, improve victim services, maintain reliable indicator sets and finally generate comparable evidence. The potential benefit depends on institutional design and resources; creating an agency does not by itself solve accuracy or legality.

## 12. The concerns, in detail

### 12.1 General and indiscriminate scanning

The central legal objection is about the *population examined*, not merely the purpose. A service may be high-risk, but most of its users and messages are innocent. If an order causes every communication on that service to be analyzed, the operation is general at user level even if the model searches only for CSAM.

The [EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 4/2022](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2022-07/edpb_edps_jointopinion_202204_csam_en.pdf) said the Commission proposal could become the basis for generalized and indiscriminate scanning of virtually all electronic communications. It found the triggering conditions too vague and argued that the interference might affect the essence of privacy where authorities obtain generalized access to content. It recommended restricting detection to concrete, individualized suspicion and protecting encrypted communications.

The Commission answers that orders are risk-based, time-limited, a last resort and issued by a court or independent body; see its explanation, [“Children deserve protection and privacy”](https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/children-deserve-protection-and-privacy-2022-05-11_en). That supplies procedural targeting at the service/order level. It does not by itself answer whether the actual content examination is targeted at people.

The Council Legal Service reached a similar conclusion in 2023: broad detection orders needed tighter personal targeting to survive Charter review. Parliament followed that approach; the Council ultimately deleted detection orders from its mandate.

### 12.2 Known hashes, perceptual hashes and AI are not one technology

Public debate often speaks of a single “scanner,” but at least three tasks differ sharply:

1. **Exact cryptographic hashes** detect a byte-for-byte copy. They have high specificity but are defeated by trivial file changes.
2. **Perceptual hashes** seek visually similar copies after cropping, resizing or compression. They broaden recall but introduce similarity thresholds, collision risks and an attack surface around the secret reference list.
3. **Machine-learning classifiers** infer whether a never-before-seen image, video or conversation depicts abuse or grooming. They must interpret age, nudity, sexual context, relationships, irony, slang, languages and conversational history.

The third task is not “hash matching with AI.” It is probabilistic content judgment. Training data are exceptionally sensitive and hard to share for independent testing. Performance may change by language, age, skin tone, culture, platform behavior and adversarial adaptation. A laboratory score does not establish performance at EU scale.

The European Parliament Research Service’s [complementary impact assessment](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EPRS_STU%282023%29740248) concluded that tools for reliably detecting new CSAM and grooming were not sufficiently mature for the Commission’s contemplated scale and that the impact assessment did not adequately resolve effectiveness and fundamental-rights questions.

### 12.3 The base-rate problem

Even a highly accurate classifier can produce many false alerts when the target is rare. As an illustration—not a measured CSAR rate—suppose 10 out of one million messages are truly reportable. A system with 90% sensitivity finds 9. If specificity is 99.9%, it also flags about 1,000 innocent messages. Only about 0.9% of alerts are then true. Human review can prevent many reports from reaching police, but it does so by exposing private content to reviewers and creating cost, delay and possible trauma.

Precision, recall, specificity and prevalence must therefore be reported together. “99% accurate” is meaningless without knowing the metric. The interim reports do not provide standardized per-technology confusion matrices or independently audited EU prevalence data.

False negatives are equally important. Offenders can crop or transform material, use coded language, move to small self-hosted services, encrypt files separately before attaching them, or avoid regulated providers. A system can burden ordinary users while sophisticated offenders route around it.

### 12.4 End-to-end encryption and client-side scanning

E2EE protects content in transit and at the provider from criminals, hostile states, insider abuse and the provider itself. Journalists, lawyers, businesses, officials, domestic-abuse survivors and children all rely on it.

Client-side scanning (CSS) checks content on an endpoint before encryption or after decryption. The transport remains encrypted, but the endpoint gains a mechanism that can inspect, classify and potentially report plaintext. The objections are structural:

- the detection logic or indicator database can be stolen, reverse-engineered or manipulated;
- a malicious update, compromised authority or coerced provider can expand the target category;
- false alerts can disclose intimate content and social graphs;
- attackers may frame a target by sending matching material;
- device compromise becomes more valuable;
- users cannot readily verify what the opaque scanner searches for; and
- offenders can encrypt content in another application before sending, while ordinary users remain exposed.

The peer-reviewed expert paper [“Bugs in our Pockets”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.07450) analyzes these systemic security risks. Hundreds of researchers made the same core points in the [July 2023 scientists’ open letter](https://csa-scientist-open-letter.org/Jul2023). The [Internet Society’s letter to EU interior ministers](https://www.internetsociety.org/open-letters/open-letter-to-eu-interior-ministers/) argues there is no technical method that preserves the normal security guarantee of E2EE while giving a provider general content-detection capability.

This does not mean all on-device safety features are equivalent. A private, user-controlled warning that never reports content is different from a mandatory system with hidden indicators and external reporting. Governance and data flow matter as much as execution location.

### 12.5 Function creep and indicator abuse

Once deployed, a content-matching infrastructure can technically search for other categories. A statute may prohibit that, but software updates and indicator lists are operational control points. Future governments may broaden legal purposes; attackers may corrupt lists; providers may reuse models for moderation or advertising.

Known-hash matching is often described as content-blind, yet the system still answers whether a user possesses or sends an item on a secret list. Security therefore requires provenance, multi-party approval, audit logs, independent testing, rapid revocation, separation of powers and a way to challenge erroneous inclusion without publishing illegal material. The Commission proposal contained technology approval and EU Centre oversight, but critics found the controls insufficiently independent and precise.

### 12.6 Consensual sexual imagery and children’s own privacy

Detection models can flag lawful adult intimacy, health images, family photographs, art, or consensually self-generated images between adolescents. Whether adolescent imagery is illegal varies with age, national law and context. Automatic systems are poor at relationship, consent and coercion.

Children are not only potential victims to be discovered; they are rights-holders who use confidential communications to seek help, explore identity and speak with doctors, counselors, friends and trusted adults. Unintended reporting can expose LGBTQ+ children, victims who are not ready to disclose, or teenagers to family, school or police intervention. Conversely, failing to detect coerced self-generated imagery can leave a victim trapped. A child-rights analysis must consider both sides, not use Article 24 as a one-directional trump.

The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child’s [General Comment No. 25](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/general-comments-and-recommendations/general-comment-no-25-2021-childrens-rights-relation) requires states to protect children from online violence while respecting privacy, expression, access to information and evolving capacities.

### 12.7 Human review is necessary but is also an intrusion

Human confirmation reduces automated reports, especially for new CSAM and grooming. It also means employees or contractors view intensely private and sometimes illegal material. That creates:

- additional disclosure of innocent content;
- risks of reviewer error and cultural bias;
- occupational trauma;
- security risks around review queues and evidence copies; and
- difficult decisions about how much conversation context must be exposed.

High-quality review requires trained staff, access controls, mental-health support, dual review for doubtful cases, reasoned decisions and audit. Smaller or decentralized services may not be able to build such operations. Outsourcing can move sensitive content to jurisdictions or contractors with weaker control.

### 12.8 Reporting quality and law-enforcement capacity

More reports do not automatically mean more rescues. Duplicate known images, incomplete account data, foreign jurisdiction, obvious spam and false alerts can overwhelm investigators and delay urgent cases. NCMEC’s 2022 actionable/informational split and the Commission’s inability to aggregate Member-State outcomes show the measurement problem.

The EU Centre is intended to triage reports, but it needs enough expert staff and strict prioritization. Useful success measures include children safeguarded, live abuse interrupted, unique series identified, time to triage, warrants obtained, prosecutions, erroneous reports and successful appeals—not raw file or report totals.

### 12.9 Lawfulness under GDPR and ePrivacy

An ePrivacy derogation does not itself settle GDPR lawfulness. Providers process content, account identifiers, possible criminal allegations, sexual-life data and children’s data. Questions include:

- the correct Article 6 lawful basis;
- Article 9 special-category conditions;
- purpose limitation and data minimization;
- automated decision-making;
- security and international transfers;
- data-subject notice, access, correction and deletion; and
- allocation of controller responsibility among provider, EU Centre, NCMEC, Europol and police.

The EDPS’s 2026 opinion identified the absence of a clear GDPR legal basis as an existing defect. “Voluntary” is especially awkward: a provider’s voluntary choice is not the same thing as a user’s freely given consent, and consent is rarely plausible as the basis for covert scanning of every participant in a conversation.

### 12.10 Effective remedy and presumption of innocence

A false match can cause content removal, account suspension, preservation of private data and a criminal report. Investigation secrecy may delay notice. Users need an accessible explanation, rapid internal review, an independent appeal and ultimately a court remedy. They may be unable to see the classifier, indicator or alleged material because disclosure could reveal investigative methods or redistribute CSAM.

This asymmetry makes ex ante safeguards unusually important. Accuracy claims and confidential audits cannot replace the individual’s right to correct a mistake. The interim implementation data show successful restorations, proving that some initial actions were wrong, while giving too little information to evaluate remedy quality.

### 12.11 Age assurance, anonymity and exclusion

Age assurance can reduce adult access to child-oriented spaces and stop children entering services with high sexual-content risk. Methods—identity documents, payment data, facial age estimation, third-party tokens or behavioral inference—create different problems:

- collecting identity data can destroy anonymity and create breach targets;
- face estimation has error and demographic-bias risks;
- children without documents or devices may be excluded;
- adults may be incorrectly excluded or forced to disclose identity;
- age gates are easily bypassed without persistent identity linking; and
- combining age proof with communications can enable profiling.

A privacy-preserving design should prove only an age band, separate the issuer from the service, avoid retention and cross-service identifiers, offer alternatives and be independently tested. Universal identity verification is not a proportionate default merely because a service can be abused.

### 12.12 Blocking and delisting

URL blocking can limit casual access when source removal is impossible, but it does not remove the material and can be bypassed. Lists can be stale, domains can host mixed lawful/illegal content, and overbroad DNS or network blocking can suppress lawful speech. Search delisting reduces discoverability but also needs precise URLs, notice, review and list security.

Source removal and victim-centered hash propagation are generally more effective. Blocking should be a last resort with judicial authorization, narrow scope, expiry, regular verification and a challenge process.

### 12.13 Market structure, open source and decentralization

Compliance costs favor the largest platforms, which already possess moderation teams, proprietary models and reporting pipelines. Small providers face legal advice, risk assessments, human review, secure evidence storage, appeals and audits. A possible 6%-of-global-turnover fine is existential even if maximum penalties are reserved for serious breaches.

Open-source and decentralized services pose attribution problems: there may be no central operator capable of reading content or receiving an order. A rule that implicitly requires centralized control can reduce architectural diversity and European competitiveness. Conversely, exempting small or decentralized systems can create displacement routes for offenders. Proportionate thresholds, shared public tools, technical assistance and duties tied to actual control are necessary.

### 12.14 Extraterritoriality and cross-border transfers

The regime applies to providers offering services in the EU, including non-EU companies. That is necessary to avoid an easy jurisdictional loophole, but enforcement against a provider with no EU infrastructure can be difficult. Providers may face conflicting laws on encryption, privacy, preservation or reporting.

Reliance on NCMEC means EU communications data may enter a US statutory reporting system before EU authorities assess it. The permanent EU Centre could increase European autonomy, but only if the final law clearly limits onward transfers, establishes controller roles and applies EU-level necessity, security and remedy standards.

### 12.15 Institutional independence and Europol access

The proposed EU Centre would both facilitate detection and assess resulting reports. Those roles can create an incentive to validate the technologies whose use the agency promotes. Independence requires a management structure that includes child-protection, data-protection, cybersecurity and fundamental-rights expertise; transparent testing; external audit; and separation between technology support and report adjudication.

The EDPB and EDPS also objected to direct or insufficiently filtered Europol access. Law-enforcement access should follow purpose limitation, competence rules, necessity and independent oversight rather than become a general data lake.

### 12.16 Democratic process, transparency and lobbying

Council compromise documents were often marked LIMITE during live negotiations, and proposed votes were withdrawn without national roll calls. Some confidentiality is normal for bargaining, but it makes a fundamental-rights proposal hard for citizens and national parliaments to scrutinize. This is why the report distinguishes official outcomes from leaked country maps.

The Commission’s public advocacy also produced a concrete data-protection violation. In September 2023 it ran an X advertising campaign promoting the CSAR while excluding audiences associated with certain political, Eurosceptic/nationalist and religious categories. On 13 December 2024, the EDPS found that the Commission had unlawfully processed political-opinion and religious-belief data without a valid basis and issued a reprimand. See the EDPS [case summary](https://www.edps.europa.eu/press-publications/publications/newsletters/newsletter-113_en). This does not determine the merits of the CSAR, but it is a legitimate governance concern about how the proposal was promoted.

The European Ombudsman has also dealt with access to Europol–Commission documents feeding into the proposal. In [cases 1372/2024/OAM and 2219/2024/OAM](https://www.ombudsman.europa.eu/decision/216184), the Ombudsman treated such exchanges as legislative documents, reinforcing the strong public interest in access. Industry, child-safety organizations, police, cryptographers and civil-liberties groups all lobbied intensely. Their evidence should be assessed on quality and disclosed interests, not accepted or dismissed by affiliation alone.

### 12.17 Evidence quality and the missing counterfactual

The Commission’s impact assessment was criticized by the Regulatory Scrutiny Board and later supplemented by EPRS. Implementation reports lack consistent denominators, accuracy tests and case outcomes. Legislators therefore do not know:

- how much detection is additional to user reports and targeted investigation;
- how many unique children are identified because of a particular technique;
- which errors never generate an appeal;
- how many offenders migrate to other channels;
- how detection changes provider investment in prevention; or
- whether the same resources would protect more children if spent on investigators, hotlines, victim support or prevention.

The absence of perfect evidence does not justify inaction, but it changes the burden. More intrusive and broader measures need more convincing evidence, narrower pilots, independent evaluation and sunset clauses.

## 13. Less intrusive and complementary approaches

No single alternative replaces every function of CSAM detection. A credible policy portfolio combines the following and measures outcomes.

### Targeted investigation

Use judicially authorized access against identified suspects, accounts, groups or devices based on reports, undercover work, financial evidence and lawful traffic-data analysis. This aligns closely with Parliament’s position and the Council Legal Service advice. It is slower to initiate than universal automated analysis but greatly reduces the innocent population examined.

### User reporting and trusted flaggers

Make reporting prominent, safe and usable inside chats; allow a victim to submit the relevant conversation with consent; support trusted child-protection hotlines; and give urgent reports priority. Reports contain context and usually have a better base rate than blind scanning. They depend on awareness, trust and a reporter who can act, so they do not find every hidden victim.

### Safety by design

Default minors’ accounts to private; prevent unsolicited adult contact; limit discovery and recommendation of minors; detect rapid high-volume contact patterns without reading content; warn about requests for intimate images; blur unsolicited media locally; provide one-tap block/report; and make location, friend lists and status private. These measures change opportunity structures rather than inspect every conversation.

Metadata and behavior can still be sensitive and require minimization. They are not a rights-free workaround, but some service-integrity signals are less intrusive than content analysis.

### Victim-centered removal

Fund hotlines and rapid source removal, let survivors register abusive material securely, propagate vetted hashes to hosting and public-content services, search the open web, and provide a durable notice-and-stay-down remedy with appeals. Prioritize identifying the first uploader and the child over counting duplicate copies.

### Law-enforcement and judicial capacity

Invest in specialized investigators, forensic laboratories, prosecutors, cross-border joint teams and trauma-informed victim services. Standardize report fields, deduplicate before dispatch, and measure time-to-rescue. A sophisticated detection system is harmful if actionable reports wait in an overloaded queue.

### Prevention and perpetrator intervention

Fund school and parent education, confidential helplines for children, programs for people concerned about their own sexual thoughts or behavior, search deterrence, offender treatment and support against sextortion. These measures address demand and harm before an image enters a hash database.

### Narrow pilots and independent technology evaluation

Before authorizing a new-CSAM or grooming system at population scale, test it on lawful, ethically sourced datasets; publish per-language and demographic performance; conduct adversarial security review; measure prevalence and positive predictive value; and use a small, time-limited, judicially bounded pilot. Independent researchers need access to meaningful evaluation without receiving CSAM.

### Privacy-preserving age signals

Where age gating is demonstrably necessary, use unlinkable age-band credentials, minimal disclosure, multiple proof options and no central identity log. Evaluate whether safer defaults for all users achieve the objective with less collection.

These approaches are complements. The sound policy question is not “scanning or nothing,” but which combination yields the most child protection per unit of privacy, security, financial and investigative cost.

## 14. Assessment and what happens next

### Bottom-line assessment

1. **Chat Control 1.0 was real law, but it was a voluntary ePrivacy derogation, not a mandatory EU detection-order regime.** Its scope could still be broad in practice because a provider could scan all communications in a participating service. It expired on 3 April 2026.
2. **The July 2026 vote did not yet restore it.** Parliament amended the Council position at second reading. Council acceptance of all amendments or a successful conciliation is still required.
3. **Chat Control 2.0 is not law.** The Commission’s broad mandatory-detection design never secured a Council mandate. Parliament converted it into targeted, judicially controlled detection and excluded E2EE. The Council deleted mandatory detection and made voluntary scanning permanent. That unresolved model choice is the heart of trilogue.
4. **Known-CSAM hashing and new-CSAM/grooming inference should not be regulated as if they have the same reliability or intrusiveness.** The evidence supports separate thresholds, audits and legal rules.
5. **Encryption is not a drafting footnote.** A duty to inspect E2EE plaintext necessarily changes which components can access or act on content. The final law must state candidly whether that capability is allowed, forbidden or targeted to a suspect endpoint under judicial control.
6. **The published evidence is too weak for confident proportionality claims.** The Commission’s own implementation report could not definitively answer proportionality. A permanent regime should not reproduce incompatible voluntary reporting; it needs common denominators, independent audits and outcome measures.
7. **Child safety and privacy are not opposites.** Weak encryption and false accusations can harm children; undetected abuse also devastates children. The most defensible settlement combines prevention and victim services with narrowly targeted, independently authorized investigation and rigorously governed known-material matching.

### Immediate procedural scenarios for the interim file

- **Council accepts all Parliament amendments:** the amended extension is adopted, signed and published; voluntary detection resumes under an E2EE exclusion until the new sunset.
- **Council rejects one or more amendments:** conciliation begins. Parliament and Council have six weeks, extendable, to agree on a joint text.
- **Conciliation fails or either institution rejects the joint text:** the extension falls and the legal gap continues.

Because the July amendments were adopted at second reading, the Council cannot simply ignore them and promulgate its 2 July text.

### What to watch in the permanent file

The decisive indicators are not the number of trilogues but the answer to these questions:

1. Will there be mandatory detection orders at all?
2. If yes, are targets individual suspects/specific suspect groups or entire high-risk services?
3. Are E2EE communications excluded in operative articles, not only a recital?
4. Does “voluntary” scanning become permanent, and what precise GDPR basis and external supervision govern it?
5. Are new-CSAM and grooming technologies independently validated before use?
6. Who controls indicators, and can providers or users challenge errors?
7. What can the EU Centre and Europol receive, retain and transfer?
8. Are age-assurance duties unlinkable and proportionate?
9. Do success metrics measure unique victims safeguarded and erroneous reports, rather than raw file volume?
10. Will the final consolidated text receive enough public scrutiny before the votes?

Until those are answered in an agreed legal text, categorical claims that the EU has either “banned encryption” or “abandoned detection” are premature.

### Glossary

| Term | Meaning in this report |
|---|---|
| **CSAM** | Child sexual abuse material. The term emphasizes that the material records or depicts abuse rather than consensual adult pornography. |
| **CSA** | Child sexual abuse. “Online CSA” includes CSAM dissemination and solicitation/grooming. |
| **CSAR** | Proposed regulation laying down rules to prevent and combat child sexual abuse, COM(2022) 209. |
| **E2EE** | End-to-end encryption: only endpoints, not the intermediary provider, can normally decrypt content. |
| **ePrivacy Directive** | Directive 2002/58/EC on privacy and electronic communications. |
| **Detection order** | An authority-issued obligation in the permanent proposal to deploy specified detection technologies; not the same as voluntary scanning under the interim regulation. |
| **Known CSAM** | Material already confirmed and represented by an identifier such as a hash. |
| **New/unknown CSAM** | Material not previously confirmed in the reference set, identified through probabilistic classification and human review. |
| **Grooming/solicitation** | Intentional conduct seeking to establish contact with a child for sexual-abuse purposes, defined by the applicable criminal-law framework. |
| **Hash** | A digital identifier. Cryptographic hashes match exact files; perceptual hashes seek similar audiovisual content. |
| **Client-side scanning** | Content analysis on a user device before encryption or after decryption. |
| **LIBE** | Parliament’s Committee on Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs. |
| **Coreper** | Committee of Member States’ Permanent Representatives; prepares Council work and can agree negotiating mandates. |
| **Trilogue** | Informal negotiation among Parliament, Council and Commission. A trilogue compromise needs formal approval by the co-legislators. |
| **Absolute/component-member majority** | A majority of all Parliament seats, including absences—not merely votes cast. It was 360 in the July 2026 second reading. |
